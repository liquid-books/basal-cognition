# Chapter 7 Quiz — It Doesn't Matter What It's Made Of

---

## Multiple Choice

**1.** Xenobots were created from which type of cells?

- A) Adult human neurons
- B) Embryonic frog skin cells
- C) Genetically modified bacterial cells
- D) Synthetic polymer chains

:::{dropdown} Answer
**B** — Xenobots are self-assembled from dissociated embryonic skin cells of *Xenopus laevis*, the African clawed frog. No genetic modification was involved.
:::

---

**2.** What is most surprising about the xenobot result?

- A) The cells moved faster than expected
- B) The cells required a completely new genome to build the organism
- C) The cells built a motile organism with no evolutionary history using an unchanged genome
- D) The experiment only worked once and could not be replicated

:::{dropdown} Answer
**C** — The genome did not change. The cells built something that had never existed in evolution, using the same DNA that would have built frog skin. Context, not genetics, drove the new form.
:::

---

**3.** Anthrobots differ from xenobots in that anthrobots are made from:

- A) Embryonic frog cells modified with new genes
- B) Adult human tracheal cells with no genetic modification
- C) Synthetic silicone material shaped to mimic cells
- D) Frog cells grown inside a human host

:::{dropdown} Answer
**B** — Gumuskaya et al. (2023) produced anthrobots from adult human airway cells. Not embryonic, not amphibian, and not genetically modified.
:::

---

**4.** What did anthrobots demonstrate when placed on damaged neural tissue?

- A) They merged with neurons and became neurons themselves
- B) They blocked further damage but did not promote repair
- C) They moved toward the damage and the neurons healed faster in their presence
- D) They had no measurable effect on the neurons

:::{dropdown} Answer
**C** — In vitro experiments showed anthrobots moving toward scratched neural tissue and the neurons healing faster in regions where anthrobots were present. The chapter notes this result is preliminary and not yet a treatment.
:::

---

**5.** "Latent morphospace" refers to:

- A) The physical space inside a cell's nucleus
- B) The set of body forms a genome could produce but that evolution never selected for
- C) The range of genetic mutations possible in a given species
- D) The maximum size limit of any organism built from a given genome

:::{dropdown} Answer
**B** — Latent morphospace is the space of possible forms permitted by a genome that evolution has never explored. Xenobots and anthrobots occupy two previously unrealized points in this space.
:::

---

**6.** The "weak" version of substrate independence claims that:

- A) Any physical medium can host a mind
- B) The organizational principles behind goal-directed behavior generalize across different materials
- C) Silicon is categorically inferior to carbon as a cognitive substrate
- D) Only biological materials can implement genuine intelligence

:::{dropdown} Answer
**B** — The weak claim says the organizational principles generalize — goal pursuit, error correction, setpoint-seeking — without claiming that any medium is equally suited for any function. This is the defensible version the chapter endorses.
:::

---

**7.** Multiple realizability, as applied to minds, argues that:

- A) Different people realize their potential differently
- B) A mind defined by functional organization could in principle be implemented in silicon or carbon
- C) The more complex the material, the richer the mind it can produce
- D) Only organisms with brains can realize genuine goals

:::{dropdown} Answer
**B** — Multiple realizability is the philosophical claim that the same functional property can be implemented in multiple different physical substrates. Applied to minds: if mental states are defined by what they do rather than what they are made of, silicon is not categorically excluded.
:::

---

**8.** The Chinese Room objection to functionalism (Searle, 1980) challenges the idea that:

- A) Cells can be goal-directed without neurons
- B) Passing a functional test for understanding guarantees genuine understanding
- C) Silicon chips can be made small enough to replace neurons
- D) Multiple realizability applies to physical systems

:::{dropdown} Answer
**B** — The Chinese Room shows a system that passes a functional test for understanding Chinese without anything inside the system actually understanding Chinese. The objection is that functional equivalence does not guarantee the inner property.
:::

---

**9.** According to the chapter, the key difference between biological error correction and AI agent error correction is:

- A) Biological systems are faster at detecting errors
- B) AI agents have more precise setpoints than cells do
- C) Biological error signals carry the stakes of survival; AI error signals do not
- D) AI agents cannot detect errors without human input

:::{dropdown} Answer
**C** — A xenobot that fails to maintain its form dissolves. A planarian that does not close its wound dies. The error signal is survival. AI agents have loss functions and deviation metrics, but no equivalent physical stakes.
:::

**10.** The chapter's main ethical implication is that:

- A) We should immediately grant AI systems legal rights
- B) Engineers should avoid building AI systems until we resolve the substrate independence debate
- C) We are scaling AI systems without measuring properties that may be morally relevant, and that gap is an engineering risk
- D) Moral consideration should remain tied to having neurons

:::{dropdown} Answer
**C** — The chapter's position is measured: we do not know whether AI systems have morally relevant properties, but we are scaling them rapidly without measuring the relevant dimensions. That is an engineering oversight with ethical consequences.
:::

## Short Answer

**11.** Explain the latent morphospace concept in plain language without using the term "morphospace." Your explanation should make it clear why xenobots are proof of the concept and not just a curiosity.

:::{dropdown} Sample Answer
Every organism's genome contains the instructions for more body forms than evolution has ever selected. The frog genome built skin cells over millions of years. Those cells contain tools — cilia, adhesion proteins, wound-repair signals — that work for building skin. But those same tools, in a new context without an embryo to guide them, can assemble into something completely different: a swimming, self-repairing creature that no frog has ever been.

This is not a trick or an artifact of the lab. It reveals something real about how genomes work. They are permission structures, not blueprints. The frog genome permitted xenobots. Evolution never explored that permission because there was no selection pressure to. The lab just changed the context and found what was always latent in the DNA.
:::

**12.** A classmate argues: "Substrate independence just means that the right software running on the right hardware can do anything biology can do. Silicon will eventually match carbon." Using the chapter's distinction between the strong and weak versions of the claim, explain what is defensible and what is overreaching in your classmate's argument.

:::{dropdown} Sample Answer
Your classmate is mixing the weak and strong versions of substrate independence. The weak version — which the chapter defends — says organizational principles like goal pursuit, error correction, and setpoint-seeking can be implemented in multiple substrates, including silicon. That much is well-supported by evidence from xenobots, soft robotics, and AI agent systems.

The strong version — that "silicon will eventually match carbon" in every relevant way — is a much bigger claim. It asserts that silicon can implement not just similar organizational principles but the full richness of biological cognition, including whatever properties underlie consciousness, genuine preference, and physical-stakes error correction. That claim is not currently supported and may not be testable in the near term.

The defensible version is: the principles generalize. The overreach is: therefore any substrate is equivalent for any purpose. Some substrates may be better suited for some functions than others, and we do not yet know what properties of biological substrates matter for the cognitive functions we most care about.
:::

**13.** The chapter says "context is part of the recipe." Using xenobots or anthrobots as your example, explain what this means mechanically — not just philosophically. What actually changed when the context changed, and what in the cell responded?

:::{dropdown} Sample Answer
When frog skin cells are inside an embryo, they receive positional signals: chemical gradients, mechanical forces from neighboring cells, and bioelectric patterns across the developing tissue. Those signals tell the cells where they are and what they should become. The cells express genes that build skin because those signals activate those gene expression programs.

When the same cells are dissociated and placed in a dish, those signals disappear. The cells are no longer receiving their positional context. The gene expression programs for skin are no longer being activated in the same way. But the cells still have cilia — built in because cilia are used to move mucus in developing skin. Those cilia are still functional. Without the embryonic context suppressing their use for locomotion, the cilia end up pointing outward and the cells start swimming.

The context change was the removal of positional and chemical signals. The cell response was the activation of latent cellular machinery that the original context had been suppressing or redirecting. The recipe changed not because the ingredients changed, but because the kitchen changed.
:::
