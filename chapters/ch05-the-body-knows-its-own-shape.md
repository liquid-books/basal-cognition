## Chapter Plan

**Core claim of the chapter:** Regeneration is homeostasis with a shape as the setpoint. The system compares current anatomy against a stored target and acts to close the gap — including the decision to stop. That stored target is physically real, editable, and not in the genome.

### Sections to write

1. **The stopping problem** — Everyone is impressed that a bisected flatworm regrows. The interesting part is that it *stops* at exactly one head, one tail, correct proportions. Growth that doesn't know when to quit has a name: cancer. Stopping requires a reference.
2. **Homeostasis, expanded** — Thermostats hold temperature. Bodies hold glucose, pH, calcium. Levin's move: bodies also hold *anatomy*. Same negative-feedback logic, vastly higher-dimensional setpoint.
3. **The anatomical setpoint** — What it means for a shape to be a target state. Error detection across a distributed tissue. How a wound is a mismatch signal.
4. **Where the setpoint lives** — Bioelectric patterns across cell membranes as a non-genetic memory layer. Gap junctions as the network. The genome supplies the hardware; the bioelectric pattern supplies the goal.
5. **Editing the setpoint** — The two-headed planarian. Alter the bioelectric pattern, not the DNA, and get a worm that regenerates two heads *indefinitely across generations of cutting*. The genome is unchanged. This is a rewritten target, and it is the most important experimental result in the book.
6. **Xenopus and the eye on the gut** — Ectopic organ induction as goal-injection rather than construction. What it proves about the level of abstraction the tissue works at.
7. **Setpoints in engineered systems** — Declarative infrastructure, convergent reconciliation loops, and self-healing systems as shallow versions of the same idea. Where they fall short: our setpoints are specified externally and cannot be learned or rewritten by the system itself.
8. **Does your AI know what 'done' looks like?** — The practical payoff. Agents without a target representation either stop too early or never stop. Termination criteria as anatomical setpoints. Diagnosing agents that "finish" work that is not finished.
